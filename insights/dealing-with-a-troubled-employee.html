Dealing with a Troubled Employee

One difficult employee can quietly cost you more than their salary — in your time, your attention and your team's morale. Here is a steady way to work through it.

Article

Almost every owner has had one: an employee who was fine, or even good, and is now a problem. Performance has slipped, attitude has turned, or their conduct is affecting people around them. The instinct is often to wait, hope it improves on its own, or avoid the conversation because it is uncomfortable. In our experience, waiting rarely fixes it and usually makes it worse — for the business and for you.

Start by naming the problem precisely. "They have a bad attitude" is hard to act on. "They have missed three deadlines this month" or "they raised their voice at a coworker twice last week" is something you can address directly and, if needed, document. Vague frustration leads to vague conversations that go nowhere.

Separate a performance problem from a conduct problem. Someone who is struggling to do the job — behind on skills, overwhelmed, or in the wrong role — usually responds to clear expectations, training and a fair chance to improve. Someone whose behavior is disruptive, dishonest or disrespectful toward others is a different situation, and tolerating it sends a message to the rest of your team about what you will accept.

Have the conversation sooner than feels comfortable. A short, direct, private conversation — what you have observed, what needs to change, and by when — is almost always better than another month of silent frustration. Bring specifics, not generalities. Listen to their side; there may be a factor you do not know about. But do not let listening turn into indefinitely postponing the decision.

Write things down as you go. Even a brief note to yourself after a conversation — the date, what was discussed, what was agreed — protects you and gives you a clear record if the situation continues. This matters most if things eventually move toward a more serious step.

Watch the effect on everyone else. Owners sometimes underestimate how much a struggling or difficult employee affects the rest of the team — the extra work others quietly absorb, the tension in the room, the sense that standards are not being held. Your best people notice how you handle your worst-fitting one.

Know when it is bigger than a conversation. If a situation involves harassment, safety, theft, or anything that could carry legal exposure, that is the point to bring in an employment attorney or HR advisor before you act, not after. The right first step in those situations is rarely a step you should take alone.

This article is general guidance, not legal advice, and it does not address the specific facts of your situation. For anything involving discipline, termination risk, or a potential legal issue, talk with an employment attorney or qualified HR advisor before acting.

Sometimes it helps to talk it through with someone outside the business. A conversation with us is a chance to think it through, not a substitute for legal advice.
